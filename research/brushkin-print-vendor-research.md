# Print/Fulfillment Vendor Research — Tiny Masterpieces (Brushkin) Postcard Wedge

**Date:** 2026-07-29
**Scope:** Postcard product only (glossy front+back, dropship to a third-party address, triggered post-payment via Stripe Connect). Books/canvas/mosaic vendors are out of scope except where a vendor covers both well.

Methodology note: research was conducted via web search across vendor pricing pages, API docs, and legal/privacy pages. Direct full-page fetches were unavailable in this environment, so findings rely on indexed search snippets of vendor documentation rather than complete page reads. Anything that could not be pinned to a specific, quotable source is explicitly marked **"unknown — needs direct vendor contact"** rather than assumed favorable. Every vendor claim below should be re-verified directly before a contract is signed.

---

## Executive Summary

Nine postcard-capable print/fulfillment vendors were researched: Lob, PostGrid, Click2Mail, Stannp, MyPostcard (direct-mail-API vendors that mail USPS-class postcards), and Prodigi, Gelato, Printful, CloudPrinter (print-on-demand/dropship vendors that ship postcards as parcel goods). All nine support glossy front+back postcards and all support a recipient address that differs from the account holder — dropship-to-third-party is inherent to every vendor's business model, not a special feature. The critical economic split is print-and-mail (USPS postcard-class postage, ~$0.50–$1.10/unit all-in) vs. print-and-parcel-ship (POD carriers, likely $2–$6/unit once shipping is included) — this matters more for unit economics than any single vendor's list price. On data privacy, **no vendor researched publishes an explicit, specific policy for images of minors uploaded by a third party (a parent) for print** — general "we don't knowingly collect data from children under 13/18" boilerplate is not the same protection and does not by itself satisfy a COPPA-first product's obligations to its own users. Weighing dropship support, absence of a fixed monthly platform fee at pilot scale, fastest integration path, and (relatively) most detailed public retention/security documentation, the primary recommendation is **PostGrid**, with **Lob** as backup (better documented security/compliance program, but its production tier appears to require a ~$260/mo platform subscription that PostGrid's pay-per-piece tier avoids). Every number in this report — especially unit economics — should be treated as directional until confirmed by a real vendor quote and a signed DPA.

## Primary Recommendation: PostGrid

**Rationale, weighted as requested:**

1. **Dropship-to-third-party (a):** Native to the product — the recipient/contact address is the fundamental unit of a PostGrid order, distinct from the paying account. No workaround needed.
2. **Data policy clarity (b):** PostGrid is the only researched vendor that markets a named compliance program (HIPAA, PIPEDA, GDPR, PCI DSS, SOC 2) specifically framed around regulated/sensitive mailings, which is a better starting posture for a COPPA-first conversation than a vendor with no compliance page at all. That said — **this is still not a minors-specific guarantee** (see Data-Privacy Risk Notes below); it only means PostGrid's sales/compliance team is more likely to already have a DPA template and a security questionnaire process ready to go.
3. **Fast integration (c):** REST API, well-documented auth (`x-api-key`), sandbox/live key separation, signed webhooks, and public example code on GitHub — low integration lift for a pilot. A free developer sandbox lets engineering start immediately without a sales call.
4. **Cost (d):** No minimum order, no setup fee, no mandatory monthly platform subscription to reach production — unlike Lob. At 1–100 postcards/month, PostGrid's pure pay-per-piece pricing (~$0.82–$0.86/postcard, print+postage bundled) avoids a fixed cost that would dominate unit economics at pilot scale.

**Caveat:** PostGrid's public postcard glossy spec (100lb standard, 120lb/UV gloss/satin behind a "Premium Paper" flag) should be confirmed to actually deliver the two-sided glossy full-bleed finish the product needs — verify with a sandbox test print before committing, not just document review.

## Backup Recommendation: Lob

**Rationale:**

1. **Dropship (a):** Equally native — same "to"-address-per-request model.
2. **Data policy (b):** Lob has the most mature, independently-verifiable security posture found in this research — completed SOC 2 Type II audits across all five Trust Services Criteria, and published security/compliance pages (GDPR, CCPA, HIPAA-supportive controls) plus a documented, concrete retention window (mailpiece data min. 33 days, deleted after 90 days) — more specific than PostGrid's public language. This makes Lob attractive as the fallback specifically because a due-diligence/DPA conversation should be easier to close.
3. **Integration (c):** Comparable REST API maturity, HMAC-signed webhooks, multiple SDKs, well-documented postcard design specs (exact bleed/trim/safe-zone dimensions publicly published, which PostGrid doesn't surface as clearly).
4. **Cost (d):** This is Lob's weak point — public evidence suggests production use requires a paid platform tier starting around **$260/month** (Small Business, up to 3,000 mailings), on top of ~$0.48–$0.90+ per postcard. At true pilot volume (1–100 orders/month) that fixed cost could dwarf the variable cost per unit, making Lob materially more expensive than PostGrid until volume grows — hence backup, not primary, despite the stronger compliance documentation. **This pricing structure needs direct confirmation** — it's possible Lob offers a lower-commitment tier for early-stage companies not reflected in the third-party pricing aggregators used here.

## Data-Privacy Risk Notes (per vendor)

- **Lob:** No published minors/children clause found anywhere in this research. Has the strongest general security program (SOC 2 Type II) of the group, which should make a bespoke DPA conversation easier, but "strong general security" is not the same as "safe for children's images" — unknown until confirmed directly.
- **PostGrid:** No specific retention period for uploaded print content found; no COPPA/minors clause found despite a fairly prominent general "Compliance" marketing page (HIPAA/PIPEDA/GDPR/PCI/SOC2) — that page's compliance claims are about regulated adult data categories (health, financial), not children's images, and should not be assumed to extend automatically. Unknown — needs direct vendor contact.
- **Click2Mail:** Retention has a real known gap — sub-users can retain cached copies of content even after a master user deletes it. That is a concrete, negative finding, not just an "unknown," and should be treated as a blocker until Click2Mail confirms a hard-deletion path exists. No children's-data clause found otherwise.
- **Prodigi:** Broad worldwide content license taken for order fulfillment (typical for POD, but worth having counsel confirm scope is limited to fulfillment and doesn't implicitly cover other uses). No AI-training or minors-specific statement found. A DPA is referenced as available but must be requested — unknown until reviewed.
- **Gelato:** Reasonably specific deletion language ("deleted once no longer necessary for the purpose collected") is a positive relative signal, but still no minors-specific or AI-training-specific statement. Unknown on those two points.
- **Printful:** The most concerning language found in this research — a "de-identified/aggregated data may be used for Printful's own R&D" clause. For a product whose entire input is children's original artwork/photos, "de-identified" is not a clean concept, and this needs explicit written clarification (ideally a contractual carve-out) before Printful could be trusted with this data category.
- **CloudPrinter:** The children's-data language found may not even be Cloudprinter.com's own policy (possible confusion with a similarly named company) — treat as unverified, not just unknown, until directly reconfirmed on Cloudprinter.com's actual legal pages.
- **Stannp / MyPostcard:** Both are unknown on the specific question (minors' images, AI training) and both carry additional integration/logistics unknowns (non-US postal infrastructure, thinner public documentation) that compound the diligence burden — lowest priority for a first vendor call.

**Cross-cutting finding:** every vendor's "children" language (where it exists at all) is framed as "we don't knowingly collect data directly from children under 13/16/18" — the standard COPPA-operator disclaimer for a company's own website users. None of it addresses the actual Tiny Masterpieces scenario: an adult uploads a child's image/artwork to a vendor as part of a paid fulfillment transaction. That is a materially different legal question (vendor-as-service-provider/processor handling a minor's personal information on behalf of a COPPA-covered operator) that essentially no vendor's public policy speaks to. This is the single biggest reason a signed DPA — not public policy language — has to be the actual gate before launch.
